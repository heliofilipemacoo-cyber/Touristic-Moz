Introduction & First Considerations

Mozambican cuisine is the product of centuries of trade and exchange: Portuguese ingredients like garlic, olive oil and piri-piri chilli, layered onto Swahili-Arab coconut, cassava and spices, layered again onto local staples like maize and peanuts. The result is bold, coastal, and — in the best way — hard to categorize.

Rather than listing every regional variation, we focus on five dishes you're genuinely likely to encounter, from street stalls to sit-down restaurants: Matapa (the national dish), Piri-Piri Chicken, grilled seafood, Xima (the everyday staple), and Rissóis de Camarão (a beloved street snack).

Two things worth knowing before you eat your way through the country: keep an eye out for achar de manga (a spiced green mango pickle) as a condiment — a small spoonful transforms almost any dish — and save room for Bolo Polana, a dense cashew-and-potato cake that's one of the country's signature desserts.

Matapa

Widely considered Mozambique's national dish — cassava leaves pounded to a paste and stewed with ground peanuts, garlic, and coconut milk until thick and creamy. Earthy, nutty, and deeply comforting.

  • Spice level: Mild
  • Where to find it: Markets, local restaurants, home kitchens
  • Price range: $
  • Dietary notes: Often contains shrimp or crab

A Bit of History

Matapa predates Portuguese colonization, rooted in the use of cassava — a crop that spread across the region centuries ago and remains a staple across much of southern and central Africa. It's traditionally eaten with the hands, scooped up with a ball of xima, and is common at both everyday meals and special occasions like weddings.

Why try it: it's the single dish most Mozambicans would point to first if asked "what should I eat here?" — skipping it means missing the closest thing the country has to a signature flavor.

Piri-Piri Chicken

Photo coming soon

Chicken marinated in a fiery piri-piri chilli sauce with garlic, lemon and herbs, then grilled over charcoal until smoky and crisp. Mozambique is widely credited as the birthplace of this dish, which later found global fame through Portuguese-African fusion cooking.

  • Spice level: Medium–Hot (ask for less if needed)
  • Where to find it: Everywhere — from street grills to restaurants
  • Price range: $–$$
  • Dietary notes: Naturally gluten-free

Variations

  • Photo coming soon
    Galinha à Zambeziana

    A milder version from the Zambézia region, marinated in coconut milk alongside the piri-piri for a subtler, creamier finish.

  • Photo coming soon
    Galinha Asada

    Simple flame-grilled chicken, letting the charcoal and chilli do the talking — found at roadside stalls across the country.

A Bit of History

The piri-piri chilli itself was brought to Mozambique by Portuguese traders from the Americas, then took root and became central to the local cuisine. The fusion of that chilli with African grilling traditions produced a dish that eventually inspired restaurant chains recognized worldwide — though nothing quite matches a version grilled roadside over real charcoal.

Why try it: this is Mozambique's most famous culinary export — tasting the original, cooked over real coals by someone who's been doing it for decades, is worth the trip on its own.

Grilled Seafood

With over 2,700 km of coastline, fresh seafood is a given in Mozambique — prawns, calamari, crab, lobster and reef fish, most often simply grilled over charcoal and served with lemon, piri-piri, or a garlic butter sauce.

  • Spice level: Adjustable (ask for piri-piri on the side)
  • Where to find it: Coastal towns, beach restaurants, markets
  • Price range: $$–$$$ (prawns can be pricier)
  • Dietary notes: Shellfish allergy — always ask

    Peri-Peri Prawns

    Mozambique's most famous seafood dish — large prawns marinated in piri-piri sauce and grilled whole, shell-on.

  • Photo coming soon
    Caril de Camarão

A Bit of History

Mozambique's coastal cuisine reflects a thousand years of Indian Ocean trade — Swahili-Arab traders brought coconut and spices long before Portuguese colonization, and later Indian and Goan communities added curry spices and techniques. Coastal towns like Vilanculos, Inhambane and Ilha de Moçambique are particularly known for fresh, simply-prepared seafood.

Why try it: few places offer seafood this fresh at this price — it's often caught the same day, a few metres from where you're eating it.

Xima

Photo coming soon

A stiff, neutral-flavoured maize porridge — Mozambique's version of pap or ugali — that accompanies almost every stew or curry in the country. Simple, filling, and eaten daily by most Mozambicans.

  • Spice level: None (it's a neutral side)
  • Where to find it: Everywhere — a daily staple
  • Price range: $
  • Dietary notes: Naturally vegan and gluten-free

Variations

  • Photo coming soon
    Mucapata

    A softer alternative from Quelimane, made with mung beans, coconut milk and rice mashed together.

  • Photo coming soon
    Xima with Caril de Amendoim

    Paired with a peanut-based stew — a common, hearty inland combination.

A Bit of History

Maize became a staple crop across southern Africa after being introduced from the Americas centuries ago, gradually replacing older grains as the region's everyday starch. Xima is eaten by hand — a small ball is pinched off, shaped into a scoop, and used to gather up whatever stew it's served with.

Why try it: it's the dish that best shows you how Mozambicans actually eat day to day — humble, but essential to understanding the rest of the cuisine.

Rissóis de Camarão

Crescent-shaped, breadcrumb-coated croquettes filled with a creamy shrimp filling, deep-fried until golden. A beloved street snack, best eaten hot and fresh from the pan at a roadside stall.

  • Spice level: Mild (sometimes with a piri-piri kick)
  • Where to find it: Street stalls, bakeries, cafés
  • Price range: $
  • Dietary notes: Contains shellfish and gluten

Variations

  • Photo coming soon
    Rissóis de Carne

    The meat-filled version, popular where seafood is less readily available.

  • Photo coming soon
    Samosas

    A common companion snack on the same stalls — another marker of Mozambique's Indian and Goan culinary influence.

A Bit of History

Rissóis originated in Portugal and travelled to Mozambique through colonization, adapting over time to use fresh local shrimp instead of the original recipe's ingredients. Today they're thoroughly Mozambican — a go-to snack for a quick, affordable bite between meals.

Why try it: it's the easiest, cheapest way to snack like a local between sightseeing stops — no restaurant required.
