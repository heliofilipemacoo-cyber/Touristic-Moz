Introduction & First Considerations

Mozambique has more than a dozen national parks and reserves, but most are either hard to reach or offer little in the way of tourist infrastructure. To keep this guide genuinely useful, we focus on four protected areas that cover the full range of what the country offers: a classic safari comeback story, an easy day trip from the capital, a marine sanctuary, and a true wilderness expedition.

Below you'll find in-depth coverage of Gorongosa National Park (Sofala, central Mozambique), Maputo National Park (a short drive from the capital), the Bazaruto Archipelago (Inhambane's marine park), and the Niassa Special Reserve (the country's largest and wildest protected area, in the far north).

One honest note before you plan: Mozambique's parks were heavily affected by the civil war (1977–1992), and wildlife numbers in most reserves are still recovering. This isn't the place for guaranteed Big Five sightings the way Kruger or the Serengeti might be — it's a place to witness genuine, ongoing conservation work, often with far fewer other visitors around.

Gorongosa National Park

Mozambique's flagship park and one of Africa's great conservation comeback stories — a reserve nearly emptied of wildlife by the civil war, rebuilt over the last two decades into a thriving, uncrowded safari destination.

  • Best time: May–November (dry season)
  • Getting there: ~3–4h drive from Beira
  • Budget: $$$
  • Malaria risk: High

Wildlife You Might See

  • Antelope & Waterbuck

    Waterbuck numbers have grown from under 500 to over 55,000 since restoration began — one of the clearest signs of the park's recovery.

  • Photo coming soon
    African Wild Dogs

    Reintroduced in 2018 after being locally extinct; more than 100 now roam the park in three packs.

A Bit of History

Before the war, Gorongosa was considered one of the finest game parks in Africa. Two decades of conflict wiped out over 90% of its large mammals. Since 2008, a long-term restoration partnership between the Mozambican government and the Carr Foundation has brought elephants back from around 200 to over 600, and lions from as few as 15 to more than 100 — one of the most closely documented ecosystem recoveries in the world.

Good to Know

The park's terrain varies dramatically in a single visit: seasonal floodplains, lake systems thick with hippos, and the cooler montane forests of Mount Gorongosa, home to species found nowhere else on Earth. Birdwatchers should take note — over 500 bird species have been recorded, more than in many entire countries.

Map of Gorongosa - Google Maps

Why go: for the story as much as the sightings — few places let you witness an ecosystem being rebuilt in real time, with knowledgeable guides who are often part of that restoration work themselves.

Maputo National Park

Formerly known as Maputo Special Reserve, this coastal park combines elephant herds, wetlands, and pristine beaches — and at just 100km from the capital, it's the most accessible wildlife experience in the country.

  • Best time: May–October (dry season)
  • Getting there: ~2–3h drive from Maputo, 4x4 needed
  • Budget: $$
  • Malaria risk: Moderate

Wildlife You Might See

  • Small Antelope

    Steenbok, duiker, and reedbuck are common across the park's grasslands and swamp forests.

  • Photo coming soon
    Nesting Sea Turtles

    Loggerhead and leatherback turtles nest on the park's protected coastline, one of few places in the region where this can still be seen.

A Bit of History

Originally proclaimed in 1932 to protect one of the last coastal elephant populations in southern Africa, the reserve was devastated during the civil war. In 2021 it merged with the neighbouring Ponta do Ouro Marine Reserve to form Maputo National Park, combining land and sea conservation under one protected area. Elephant numbers have since recovered to around 400, helped by herds migrating in from South Africa's Tembe Elephant Park.

Good to Know

The park's mix of dune forest, wetlands, and mangroves means a single visit can combine a game drive with dolphin or whale-shark spotting offshore. Roads are mostly soft sand — a 4x4 is genuinely necessary, not just recommended.

Map of Maputo National Park - Google Maps

Why go: it's the only place in Mozambique where you can see elephants and whale sharks on the same trip, without needing to fly anywhere or budget extra travel days.

Bazaruto Archipelago

Mozambique's oldest marine park, and home to the last viable population of dugongs in the entire western Indian Ocean — five islands of dunes, coral reefs, and some of the clearest water on the East African coast.

  • Best time: April–December
  • Getting there: Boat or light aircraft from Vilanculos
  • Budget: $$$–$$$$
  • Malaria risk: Moderate

Wildlife You Might See

  • Photo coming soon
    Dugongs

    Around 250–300 remain — the last viable population in the western Indian Ocean. Guided, distance-limited encounters only, to protect the species.

  • Photo coming soon
    Coral Reefs

    Over 2,000 fish species, five nesting turtle species, and regular manta ray and whale shark sightings.

A Bit of History

Declared a protected area in 1971, Bazaruto is Mozambique's first and oldest marine park. Since 2017, the conservation NGO African Parks has managed the reserve, working to curb illegal fishing and unregulated tourism that had put the archipelago's ecosystems at risk.

Good to Know

Three of the five islands are inhabited, with a mix of local fishing communities and a handful of exclusive lodges. It's one of the pricier nature destinations in the country, but also one of the most rewarding for snorkelling and diving.

Map of Bazaruto Archipelago - Google Maps

Why go: for wildlife you genuinely can't see almost anywhere else on Earth — dugongs are functionally extinct across most of their former range, and this is one of the last strongholds left.

Niassa Special Reserve

Photo coming soon

Africa's third-largest wildlife sanctuary — bigger than Switzerland, and almost entirely without tourist infrastructure. This is the least visited, most genuinely wild part of Mozambique's nature offering.

  • Best time: May–December
  • Getting there: Charter flight from Lichinga or Pemba
  • Budget: $$$$
  • Malaria risk: High

Wildlife You Might See

  • Photo coming soon
    Elephants

    Niassa holds Mozambique's largest elephant population by far — though the reserve's size and lack of habituation mean sightings take patience.

  • Photo coming soon
    Wild Dogs & Lions

    One of the most important strongholds for African wild dogs on the continent, alongside a recovering lion population.

A Bit of History

At over 42,000 km², Niassa is connected by a natural wildlife corridor to Tanzania's Nyerere National Park (formerly Selous), together forming one of Africa's largest contiguous wilderness areas. Unlike Gorongosa, it was largely spared the worst of the civil war due to its remoteness, so its wildlife populations stayed comparatively intact.

Good to Know

There are no rhinos here — they were lost decades ago — and animals in general tend to be skittish due to nearby hunting concessions, so this is not a place for guaranteed close-up sightings. What it offers instead is scale and solitude: granite domes rising hundreds of metres over the canopy, and days where you may not see another vehicle.

Map of Niassa Special Reserve - Google Maps

Manage your expectations: Niassa is for adventure and conservation enthusiasts, not casual wildlife viewing — it takes real planning, a bigger budget, and a tolerance for uncertainty. If that sounds appealing rather than off-putting, this is one of the last true wildernesses left in Africa.
